A product person who loves building things, and loves working with people even more

I study materials and systems engineering at UVA. So far I've shipped an app, designed a wearable and its charging case, packaged a phone-mounted timekeeper for a manufacturer's review, and printed a desk display.

By Albert Sein   |   Charlottesville, Va.

I am doing an accelerated BS in Materials Science and an MEng in Systems Engineering, finishing in 2027 and 2028.

This summer I was a project management and thermal engineering intern at Celestica, working on next generation data center infrastructure for AI. Most of it was on the TIMA5, running conductivity tests on liquid metal thermal interface materials: thermal impedance, warpage, heat maps of where a package actually stays hot. ApertureHardwareAlbert Sein

Hardware Wearable

Aperture

A wearable for your mind. It rests in the groove behind your ear: a crescent of dark brushed aluminium on a soft silicone base. I worked on the mechanical design, the electronics integration and the charging case.

2026   Wearable   Fusion 360   KiCad

Visit Aperture ↗

Shaped to the head

The shell's face follows the curve of the head along its length, so it stays the same thickness from tip to tip instead of lifting off at the ends.

Electronics, folded to the curve

The board is rigid-flex in three sections, each sitting tangent to the shell's curve and joined by short flex hinges. Steel rings soldered to it pass through the floor to meet the magnets in the base. Part positions come back from the KiCad layouts into the CAD, and every rebuild reruns the interference check.

One file, three test tracks

Every dimension lives in one parameters file that rebuilds the drawings, the fit checks, the Fusion 360 model and both board outlines. The build and test plan runs three tracks side by side: build the first units, scan heads, and run bench and wear tests, with results feeding back into that file before anything is tooled.

The charging case

I designed the case around the shell: a crescent base and a lid on a pin hinge. Magnets pull the shell into its cups, and it charges through the same steel rings that hold it to the base.

Its own board and battery

Under the pocket sit the case's own board, cell and USB-C port. A Hall sensor watches the lid, and the contacts only switch on with the lid shut and a shell drawing current.

Drop it in, close the lid

The shell settles into its cups, the lid swings shut on its pin hinge, and a magnet in the lid's front edge holds it closed while it charges.

SnackHardwareAlbert Sein

Hardware Consumer device

Snack

A physical timekeeper that sits on the back of your phone. One click starts a Snack Break and blocks the apps you chose; a second click ends it, and the app keeps an honest record of where your time went.

2026   Consumer hardware   Fusion 360

Under 8 mm

Revision R03 threw out the 9.8 mm stack and started clean at 64 by 78 by 7.6 mm. Every part had to fit a named volume after walls, corner radii, adhesive and battery swell, with 7.8 mm as a hard limit.

Two boards in 7.6 mm

A main board with the USB-C port, a display board joined to it by a flex, the cell, the attachment ring and a deadfront glass, stacked inside the frame. A scripted audit checks 83 critical fits, down to 0.15 mm between the USB-C connector and its cage.

Made for the back of an iPhone

The attachment ring sits on the MagSafe axis from Apple's accessory drawing, so Snack lands in the same place on every iPhone 17 Pro. Its top edge stops about 4 mm short of the camera plateau, and the top 10 mm is a nonconductive cap that keeps the metal frame out of the zone Apple asks accessories to leave clear.

Snaps on, then lights up

The magnets take it the last few millimetres. The face is dead front: 512 warm-white LEDs in a 32 by 16 grid sit behind the glass, invisible until they light. Here they confirm the snap, then run a break timer.

Ready for a manufacturer's review

R07 is packaged for an OEM design-for-manufacture review: a BOM, a source ledger for every part, an interface net table, and a release checklist where nothing closes without an owner, an artifact, a date and an approver.

Hardware Desk display

Ordo Desktop

A desk display for Ordo, my calendar app. A hull, a base plate, and a screen set into the face.

Spring 2026   Fusion 360   PLA

Two prints and a screen

The hull and the base plate print separately on a Prusa MK4S, about five hours between them at 0.3 and 0.2 mm layers.

Sized for a desk

186 mm wide, 68 mm tall and 114 mm deep, with the base plate running out past the hull as a front ledge.

Smaller builds

Briefs

From the archive High school

Project Skyfall

A model rocket I designed in high school that steers with its motor. Two SG90 servos tilt a gimbal to point the thrust, at the tail of a 75 mm PE pipe airframe under a nose cone. This is the second prototype, about 54 cm long.

High school   Fusion 360   Prototype 2

Bottomless portafilter

A replica of a bottomless portafilter, modeled around a 58 mm basket, with two locking lugs instead of three. I scaled it down to fit a two-inch cube for the print.

February 2026   MSE 2200

Topology optimization

A Ti-6Al-4V strut between two fixed platforms, optimized in Fusion 360 for stiffness to weight under a 5,000 N load, with a safety factor of about 3.45.

April 2026   MSE 2200
