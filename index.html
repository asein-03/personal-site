Now

Building a brain-computer interface

What we've built and learned on Aperture so far.

Aperture is a brain-computer interface that rests behind the right ear and reads brain signal through three dry electrodes. A model in the phone app turns that signal into readings of sleep and alertness. I co-founded it and lead the product.

I came to it with no neurotech background. Before our own device records anything, we used public brain recordings to learn what the ear position can and can't predict, and designed the hardware around the answer.

  • Neural networksTrained compact convolutional networks in PyTorch on 20 public EEG datasets: 917 people and 5,232 hours. One channel behind the ear scored sleep stages as well as a clinical scalp channel (κ = 0.77).
  • What the ear can readDrowsiness at an AUC of 0.82 over 90 seconds, and eyes-closed rest at 81%. Gating by head movement cut false readings during exercise from 23–48% to 1–11%. Mind wandering and mental effort stayed at chance, so we don't claim them.
  • Learning architectureA 14-channel EPOC+ headset teaches a smaller model that sees only the behind-ear sites, scored on people and days it has never seen.
  • Aperture LabA Mac app for our first experiment: it records the behind-ear sites and the EPOC+ in sync, runs guided sessions with timed cues, and trains and scores models on what it records.
  • HardwareThe wearable in Fusion 360: a sealed pod 4.45 mm thick and 3.77 g on a skin frame changed weekly, a curved four-layer flex board with 77 parts laid out in KiCad, and a pebble-shaped charging case with its own board.
