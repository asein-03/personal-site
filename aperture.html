Aperture

A brain-computer interface you wear behind your ear. I co-founded it and lead the product. We train the neural networks that read its signal, and I design the device and its charging case.

Role
Co-founder and product lead; mechanical design, electronics integration, charging case
When
June 2026 to now
Tools
PyTorch, Fusion 360, KiCad
Site
wearaperture.com ↗

What I did

  • Trained compact neural networks in PyTorch on 20 public EEG datasets (917 people, 5,232 hours) to learn what two behind-ear channels can predict.
  • Designed the learning architecture: a 14-channel EPOC+ headset teaches a smaller model that sees only the device’s two channels, evaluated on people and days the model never saw.
  • Built Aperture Lab, a Mac app that records the behind-ear sites and the EPOC+ in sync, runs guided sessions with timed cues, and trains and scores models on the recordings.
  • Designed the wearable and its charging case in Fusion 360 from one parameters file, with both boards laid out in KiCad and their part positions fed back into the CAD.
  • Wrote the R&D plan to design freeze in May 2027, the first study’s protocol and consent form, and a research paper on near-ear sensing.

The models

What we asked

Before our own device records anything, we used recordings other labs have published to learn which states two short channels behind one ear can predict, in people a network has never seen.

What came through

Sleep, drowsiness, eyes-closed rest and physical activity. A channel behind the ear scored sleep as well as a clinical one, and gating the electrodes by head movement cut false readings during exercise from 23–48% to 1–11%.

What didn’t

Mind wandering, mental effort and stress were at chance once elapsed time and session were controlled for, even with a 14-sensor headset. So we don’t build the product on them.

Teacher and student

For our own data, a 14-channel EPOC+ headset recorded at the same time teaches a smaller model that sees only the device’s two channels. Aperture Lab, the Mac app I built for it, records both in sync and scores each model on people and days held out.

The wearable

Shaped to the head

The shell’s face follows the curve of the head along its length, so on the head it’s made for the whole stack is 7.1 mm thick from tip to tip, instead of lifting off at the ends. It sits on a soft silicone base with dry electrodes.

Electronics, folded to the curve

The board is rigid-flex in three sections, each sitting tangent to the shell’s curve and joined by short flex hinges. Steel rings soldered to it pass through the floor to meet the magnets in the base. Part positions come back from the KiCad layouts into the CAD, and every rebuild reruns the interference check.

One file, three test tracks

Every dimension lives in one parameters file that rebuilds the drawings, the fit checks, the Fusion 360 model and both board outlines. The build and test plan runs three tracks side by side: build the first units, scan heads, and run bench and wear tests, with results feeding back into that file before anything is tooled.

The charging case

Off the base

To charge it, the shell comes off its silicone base. The steel rings hold on to the base’s magnets for a moment, then let go, and the base stays where it is.

Designed around the shell

A crescent base and a lid on a pin hinge. Magnets pull the shell into its cups, and it charges through the same steel rings that hold it to the base, much like earbuds in their case.

Its own board

Under the pocket sits the case’s own board, laid out in KiCad, with its USB-C port set just inside the curved back wall so only the port shows from outside.

Drop it in, close the lid

The shell settles into its cups and the lid swings shut on its pin hinge. Power reaches the wearable’s board straight through its steel rings, so there are no spring contacts.
